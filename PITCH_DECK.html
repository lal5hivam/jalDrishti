The problem / scale

India runs on water
it cannot see

63%
of irrigated area

Fed from groundwater, not surface canals or rivers.

85%
of rural drinking water

Drawn from wells and borewells across the country.

1st
largest user, worldwide

India extracts more groundwater than any other nation.

The resource is invisible, so it is managed reactively. A district usually learns its water table has fallen only after wells begin to fail.

The problem / the data

The measurements exist.
They just can't be read.

The Central Ground Water Board monitors a national network of observation wells and publishes every reading, every year — as hundreds of pages of tabulated PDF.

  • A PDF table cannot be queried, ranked, or joined to anything else.
  • Nothing in the document compares one region against another.
  • Nothing in it looks forward — there is no station-level forecast.
  • The raw depth figure isn't comparable: 10 m is routine in one aquifer and alarming in the next.
The system / five layers

LAYER 02

Cleaning, quality control, and a per-station historical baseline.
→ clean set + baselines
LAYER 03
Analytics
The GAVI score, then a prioritised alert engine over score and trend.
→ scored + alerted
LAYER 04
Forecasting
One- and three-year projections per station, converted back into scores.
→ forward alerts
LAYER 05
Presentation
An interactive dashboard built for officials, not analysts.
→ live dashboard

Each layer persists its own dataset, so any stage can be re-run and refined without repeating the expensive ones upstream.

Covering 2015 to 2024. January readings are used throughout, so every station is compared against itself at the same point in the annual recharge cycle — the seasonal swing is removed by construction, not by modelling.

The index / core contribution

GAVI: one number
that travels

GAVI = 100 × [ 1 − ( WL_current − WL_min ) ÷ ( WL_max − WL_min ) ]

Each station is rescaled against its own historical extremes. 100 is that station at its best on record; 0 is its worst.

Because the normalisation is station-specific, local geology and aquifer type are absorbed into the baseline — which is what makes a score of 30 in Rajasthan mean the same thing as a score of 30 in Odisha.

A district officer reads a category and an action — never a hydrological unit.

Alerts / resolved by priority

One station,

GAVI below 50 and the table is falling
03
SUDDEN_DROP
Level falls 2.0 m or more in one year
04
RECOVERY_SIGNAL
Level rises 1.0 m or more in one year
05
NORMAL
No rule satisfied; routine monitoring

The ladder is strictly ordered — severity always outranks trend, so no station can carry contradictory signals.

Alerts / trust

An alert nobody
believes is worthless

A single bad reading — a faulty gauge, one anomalous season — must not put a district into emergency response. Two filters stand between the data and the siren:

2×
Persistence check

A critical alert must appear in two or more consecutive observations before it is treated as confirmed.

3obs
Rolling window

A three-observation window smooths transient spikes so measurement noise cannot trigger a response.

Forecast / the model

Deliberately simple,
on purpose

WL_future = WL_last + mean historical ΔWL

Each station is projected at one-year and three-year horizons, clipped to the physically meaningful range of 0–50 mbgl, then converted back into a GAVI score against the same baseline.

Two reasons the first model is a transparent trend and not a neural network: it can be explained to the official acting on it, and it sets the benchmark any machine-learning model has to beat to justify its opacity.
